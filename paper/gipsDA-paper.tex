\title{gipsDA: An R Package for Discriminant Analysis Regularized by Projection onto Cone Invariant Under Action of the Underlying Subgroup of The Symmetry Group}


\author{by Adam Chojecki, Norbert Frydrysiak, and Antoni Kingston}

\maketitle

\abstract{%
Statistical classification is a fundamental problem in machine learning and data analysis, with applications ranging from genomic sequencing and medical diagnosis to financial forecasting. Among the vast landscape of classification algorithms, generative models such as Linear and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (LDA and QDA) remain cornerstones of the field due to their rigorous statistical foundations, interpretability, and computational efficiency. However, nowadays these classical methods are frequently confronted with high-dimensional datasets, scenarios where theby number of features rivals or exceeds the number of observations. In such environments, standard estimation techniques often fail, necessitating the development of more robust approaches. We propose a novel solution to this challenge by harnessing the concept of invariance under the action of a subgroup of the symmetric group for the task of discriminant analysis. Specifically, we explore how identifying and enforcing permutation symmetries within the data can serve as a form of model-based regularization, stabilizing covariance estimation without resorting to arbitrary shrinkage. \textbf{Keywords:} Classification, Discriminant Analysis, High-dimensional, Regularization, Permutation Symmetries, Covariance Estimation
}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Well-established generative classifiers, such as Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), remain fundamental tools in statistical machine learning \citep{elemstats} due to their interpretability and closed-form solutions.
However, the performance of these methods is critically dependent on accurate estimation of class-conditional covariance matrices, denoted as \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\).
In the current state of affairs it is not uncommon for researchers to encounter high-dimensional datasets where the number of features (\(p\)) is large relative to the number of available observations (\(n\)).
In such settings, standard estimators often fail, rendering classical methods suboptimal or entirely inapplicable.

The bottleneck of the aforementioned classifiers lies in the sample covariance matrix, \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\).
For a dataset with \(p\) features, the covariance matrix contains \(p(p+1)/2\) unique parameters that must be estimated.
This implies a quadratic relation between model's complexity and the dimension of the feature space.
The relation leads to two primary challenges:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Parameter Instability:} When \(p\) is large, the number of parameters to estimate can easily exceed the information content available in the training data. This results in estimates with high variance, leading to overfitting and poor generalization performance on unseen data.
\item
  \textbf{Estimator Singularity:} In extreme regimes where the number of features exceeds the sample size (\(p > n\)), the sample covariance matrix \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) becomes rank-deficient. Consequently, it is no longer positive definite and, as such, not invertible. Since the decision functions for both LDA and QDA require computation of the precision matrix \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\), classical algorithms break down in these scenarios.
\end{enumerate}

One way of mitigating these are various regularization techniques.
While standard approaches often involve scalar shrinkage (e.g., shrinking towards the identity matrix) \citep{friedman1989regularized}, \citep{ledoit2004well}, this project explores a structural approach.
We explore whether by identifying and enforcing permutation symmetries within the data --- where certain variables are to be exchangeable --- one can significantly reduce the number of free parameters required to estimate the covariance structure.

The assumption that distinct features in a dataset share a permutation symmetry---meaning they remain invariant under specific reorderings---may appear (and often is) overly restrictive \citep{andersson1998symmetry}. In classical multivariate analysis, features are usually treated as distinct entities with unique variances and pairwise correlations. However, in the context of high-dimensional data, the assumption of symmetry is not only mathematically convenient but often empirically justified by the underlying nature of data itself.

The core may be the concept of \emph{exchangeability}. In many datasets, features are not arbitrary measurements but rather collections of comparable units. Consider following domains where symmetry naturally arises:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Genomics and Bioinformatics:} In gene expression data, thousands of genes are measured simultaneously \citep{dudoit2002comparison}. Genes belonging to the same biological pathway or functional group often exhibit similar behavior. It is statistically plausible to assume that correlation between any pair of genes within such a functional cluster is roughly equivalent, regardless of their specific labels.
\item
  \textbf{Medical Diagnostics and Imaging:} Consider an analysis of digitized images for cancer diagnosis, such as Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset used in \citep{breast}. Features are computed from the characteristics of cell nuclei present in the image (e.g., radius, texture, smoothness) \citep{street1993nuclear}. While each feature measures a distinct geometric property, groups of features derived from similar morphological aspects often display strong, structured correlations. Assuming a symmetric covariance structure among these related biometrics allows the model to capture the general ``shape'' of malignant cells without overfitting to the noise inherent in individual sample measurements.
\item
  \textbf{Industrial Sensor Systems:} In predictive maintenance and fault detection, systems are often monitored by multiple sensors recording analogous physical quantities. A prime example is the Sensorless Drive Diagnosis dataset \citep{dataset_for_sensorless_drive_diagnosis_325}, which involves electric current signals from different phases of a motor \citep{seera2014classification}. In a balanced three-phase system, the physical properties of the phases are designed to be identical. Consequently, the statistical relationship between Phase A and Phase B should theoretically be invariant to the relationship between Phase B and Phase C. Treating these signals as exchangeable via permutation symmetry reflects the physical reality of the hardware design.
\end{itemize}

Furthermore, the \CRANpkg{gips} approach utilized by our package does not require prior knowledge of these specific relationships. Unlike rigid regularization methods that force the covariance matrix towards a specific target (such as the identity matrix), \CRANpkg{gips} methodology aims to discover the optimal symmetry structure from the data itself \citep{JSSv112i07}.

We introduce and implementat a novel regularization framework for Discriminant Analysis, designed for the ``High Dimension, Low Sample Size'' (HDLSS) regime.

\section{Theoretical Underpinnings}\label{theoretical-underpinnings}

\subsection{Linear and quadratic discriminant analysis}\label{linear-and-quadratic-discriminant-analysis}

Classical LDA and QDA model class-conditional distributions as multivariate Gaussians \(\mathrm{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu}_g,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_g)\) and assign a new observation \(\mathbf{x}\) to the class maximizing the posterior probability, or equivalently the discriminant score \citep{elemstats, venables2002}. LDA assumes a common covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_g\equiv\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) for all classes \(g=1,\ldots,m\), so that the decision boundaries are linear in \(\mathbf{x}\) and estimation reduces to class means together with a single pooled sample covariance. QDA relaxes the homoscedasticity assumption and estimates a separate \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_g\) in each class, yielding quadratic boundaries at the price of many more covariance parameters. In both cases the plug-in rules depend on invertible covariance estimates, which is precisely where the permutation-invariant projections from \CRANpkg{gips} become useful.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The \CRANpkg{gips} methodology}{The  methodology}}\label{the-methodology}

The methodology of \CRANpkg{gipsDA} builds on the theory of Gaussian models invariant under permutation symmetry, as developed by \citet{10.1214/22-AOS2174} and implemented in the \CRANpkg{gips} package \citep{JSSv112i07}. We summarize only the ingredients needed later; for proofs, conjugacy results, and algorithmic details we refer the reader to those sources.

Let \(Z^{(1)},\ldots,Z^{(n)}\) be an i.i.d.~sample from the centered Gaussian distribution \(\mathrm{N}_p(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), and let \(\mathfrak{S}_p\) denote the symmetric group on \(\{1,\ldots,p\}\). For a subgroup \(\Gamma\subset\mathfrak{S}_p\), the model is said to be \emph{invariant under} \(\ \Gamma\) if
\[
g\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\cdot g^\top = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}
\quad\text{for all } g\in\Gamma,
\]
where each permutation is identified with its permutation matrix. Equivalently, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) belongs to the \emph{colored space}
\[
\mathcal{Z}_{\Gamma}
:=
\bigl\{
S\in\mathrm{Sym}(p;\mathbb{R})
:
S_{i,j}=S_{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)}
\text{ for all }\sigma\in\Gamma
\text{ and all }i,j
\bigr\}
\]
and to the corresponding \emph{colored cone} of positive definite matrices \(\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}:=\mathcal{Z}_{\Gamma}\cap\mathrm{Sym}^+(p;\mathbb{R})\).

Any symmetric matrix \(S\) can be projected onto \(\mathcal{Z}_{\Gamma}\) by group averaging,
\[
\pi_{\Gamma}(S)
:=
\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}
\sum_{\sigma\in\Gamma}
\sigma\cdot S\cdot\sigma^\top.
\]
The projected matrix inherits the equality constraints induced by \(\Gamma\), which reduces the number of free covariance parameters. In particular, when \(\Gamma=\langle\sigma\rangle\) is cyclic, the maximum likelihood estimator of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) under this constraint exists as soon as the sample size exceeds the number of cycles of \(\sigma\) (counting in cycles of length 1), which can be substantially smaller than \(p\) \citep{JSSv112i07}.

Model selection over candidate symmetries is carried out in a Bayesian framework. Placing a uniform prior on the set of cyclic subgroups of \(\mathfrak{S}_p\) and a Diaconis--Ylvisaker conjugate prior on the precision matrix \citep{diaconis1979conjugate} yields an explicit posterior over permutation structures \citep{10.1214/22-AOS2174}. The \CRANpkg{gips} package returns either the maximum a posteriori (MAP) cyclic subgroup or an approximation of the full posterior; the empirical covariance is then replaced by its projection onto the corresponding invariant cone. For \(p\le 9\) the search can be exhaustive, while for larger \(p\) a Metropolis--Hastings sampler is used \citep{JSSv112i07}. Multi-sample Bayesian selection over several covariance matrices that share one permutation structure is also available in \CRANpkg{gips} (version \(\ge 1.3.0\)).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Models in \CRANpkg{gipsDA}}{Models in }}\label{models-in}

The package wraps the classical LDA and QDA rules around covariance estimators obtained from \CRANpkg{gips}. In each case one (i) forms the relevant empirical covariance matrix or matrices, (ii) selects a cyclic permutation structure by MAP estimation or by posterior-weighted averaging, and (iii) replaces the empirical matrix by its projection onto the corresponding invariant cone. The multi-sample selection formerly developed as a separate \texttt{gipsmult} backend is now part of \CRANpkg{gips}; \CRANpkg{gipsDA} only consumes that interface when a joint symmetry across classes is required.

Three classifiers are provided.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{gipslda()}} follows LDA: class-specific sample covariances are first pooled into one matrix and then projected under a single shared permutation structure. Pooling may use the classical unbiased estimator \(\sum_g(n_g-1)S_g/(n-m)\) (the default) or a size-weighted average \(\sum_g n_g S_g/n\); this choice concerns how class covariances are combined \emph{before} projection and should not be confused with posterior averaging over permutations. The projected matrix is then used as the common \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) in the LDA decision rule.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{gipsqda()}} follows QDA most closely: \CRANpkg{gips} is applied independently within each class, so every class may receive its own covariance estimate and its own symmetry. This is the most flexible of the three models.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{gipsmultqda()}} keeps class-specific covariances, as in QDA, but estimates one permutation structure jointly from all classes through the multi-sample interface of \CRANpkg{gips}. Classes may therefore differ in scale while sharing a common pattern of feature exchangeability.
\end{itemize}

In terms of model nesting, \texttt{gipsqda()} is the least constrained, \texttt{gipslda()} the most constrained, and \texttt{gipsmultqda()} occupies the intermediate regime of heteroscedastic covariances with a common symmetry. Practical fitting options shared by all three functions---brute-force versus Metropolis--Hastings search, MAP versus posterior averaging over symmetries, and related controls---are described in the next section.
